the story in short

Origins

holiness roots

Charles Harrison Mason, born in Shelby County, Tennessee, to formerly enslaved parents, began as a Baptist preacher. With Charles Price Jones, he preached holiness and sanctification — and was pushed out of the Baptist convention for it.

a name given

In 1897, Mason and Jones organized a new fellowship in Lexington, Mississippi, where revivals had been held in an abandoned cotton gin. Mason gave it the name he believed God had shown him: the Church of God in Christ.

to the world

Centered in Memphis under Mason's leadership for more than five decades, COGIC grew into the largest African American Pentecostal denomination — millions of members in more than a hundred countries.

timeline

1895 — today
1895

Mason meets Jones

Mason joins forces with Mississippi preacher Charles Price Jones. Together they preach holiness and sanctification across the South, until the Baptist convention expels them for it.

1897

A fellowship in Lexington

Revivals in an abandoned cotton gin in Lexington, Mississippi, gather some sixty converts. Mason and Jones organize the fellowship that takes the name Church of God in Christ, and it is incorporated in Memphis.

1906 – 1907

Azusa Street changes everything

Mason travels to the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles, led by William J. Seymour, and receives the baptism of the Holy Spirit. His Pentecostal teaching splits the fellowship in 1907 — Mason keeps the COGIC name and charter, reorganizes the church in Memphis that November, and leads it as a Pentecostal body.

1924

A temple for the convocation

COGIC builds its first National Temple in Memphis to host the annual Holy Convocation. The wooden temple burns in 1936 — and the church decides to rebuild in steel and stone.

1945

Mason Temple dedicated

The new Mason Temple is dedicated as the church's international headquarters — financed by everyday members: sharecroppers, cotton pickers, and domestic workers who gave what they had.

1961

The founder goes home

Bishop Mason dies on November 17, 1961. The movement he led for 54 years has grown to more than 400,000 members and over 4,000 churches, with congregations across the world.

1968

"I've Been to the Mountaintop"

On April 3, 1968, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. delivers his final speech at Mason Temple, standing with Memphis sanitation workers on strike. He is assassinated the next day.

Today

The fire keeps spreading

COGIC remains headquartered at Mason Temple in Memphis, gathers thousands each year for Holy Convocation, and counts millions of members in more than 100 countries — the largest African American Pentecostal church in the world.

mason temple

Memphis, Tennessee

Built between 1940 and 1945, in the middle of a world war and a shortage of building materials, Mason Temple was once the largest Black-owned structure in Memphis. It was paid for by the people of the church — many of them poor — who believed the movement deserved a house.

"Well, I don't know what will happen now. We've got some difficult days ahead. But it really doesn't matter with me now, because I've been to the mountaintop."
— Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., Mason Temple, April 3, 1968

The temple still serves as COGIC's international headquarters and the home of its Holy Convocation — a room where revival history and civil rights history share the same walls.

the legacy

Seal · Sound · Spirit

the seal

COGIC's seal pictures a shaft of wheat — the members of the church. The rope binding the shaft is Mason, who held them together. The rain behind it is the Latter Rain revival that gave birth to the Pentecostal movement.

the sound

COGIC sanctuaries became conservatories of gospel music. The Clark Sisters, the Winans family, and generations of church musicians carried the COGIC sound — Hammond organ, tambourine, and soaring choir — into the wider world.

the spirit

At the center, unchanged since Azusa Street: a Pentecostal faith in the baptism of the Holy Spirit, holiness of life, and a church where everybody — sharecropper or scholar — has a testimony.
